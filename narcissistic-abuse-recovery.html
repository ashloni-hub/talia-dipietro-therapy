What Is Narcissistic Abuse Recovery?

Narcissistic abuse recovery is therapy for what a manipulative relationship leaves behind. The focus is not on diagnosing the other person. It is based on self-doubt, shame, and the loss of your own sense of who you are.

These dynamics run on gaslighting, blame, and pressure to manage another person’s moods. Over time that produces a specific pattern: questioning your own memory, walking on eggshells, cutting yourself off from people, and believing you are the problem.

The work covers grief as well as recovery. There is the loss of the relationship, the loss of the version of yourself who existed inside it, and grief for what you never got to have. All three count.

how it works

How I Approach This Work

The dynamic gets named. We work with what the relationship was doing to you, in plain terms, rather than treating your reactions as though they came out of nowhere.

Your instincts get rebuilt. Much of the early work is restoring your trust in your own read on a situation, because that is usually what took the heaviest damage of all.

Grief has a place here. Alongside the recovery there is real loss to sit with, including the loss of who you were before it and the future you had assumed you would get.

You do not have to have left. Plenty of my clients are still in contact, still co-parenting, or still deciding what to do. The work does not require a clean break first.

Practical, not only insight. We rehearse the hard conversations, plan ahead for the visits, and build boundaries that you can hold when the pressure is turned up on you.

What I Help With

Recovery after a partner. Work on the self-doubt, shame, and hypervigilance that tend to follow a controlling or manipulative relationship.

Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is narcissistic abuse in a relationship?

It describes a pattern of relating that runs on manipulation, blame-shifting, and control of another person’s reality. The term names the dynamic. It is not a diagnosis I can give to someone who is not my client, and none of this work depends on labelling them at all.

How do I recover from a narcissistic relationship?

Recovery generally means rebuilding trust in your own perception, grieving what was lost, and learning to hold boundaries under pressure. The order varies. For many people the self-doubt lifts well before the grief does, and both parts respond to therapy that names the dynamic.

What is a trauma bond, and why is it so hard to leave?

It describes the strong attachment that can form when affection and harm come from the same person on an unpredictable schedule. It explains why leaving is so much harder than it looks from the outside. This is a recognized pattern, not a weakness on your part.

Why do I question my own memory after the relationship?

Because your account of events was contradicted often enough that doubting yourself became a habit. It is one of the most common effects clients describe to me. It tends to improve as you start checking your perceptions against reality in a setting where they are taken seriously.

Can therapy help if I still talk to the person who hurt me?

Yes. Many of my clients are still in contact by choice, by circumstance, or because they share children. We work on what you can control: your boundaries, your expectations going into contact, and your recovery afterward. Leaving is not a prerequisite here.
