What Are Emotionally Immature Parents?

An emotionally immature parent cannot reliably tolerate or respond to another person’s feelings, including their child’s. The child adapts by managing the parent instead, and that arrangement usually carries on well into adulthood.

In practice it looks like conversations that circle back to them wherever they start, out-of-proportion guilt whenever you say no, and repair attempts that lead nowhere. The parent is often still doing it now, which is what makes this different from historical work.

This is not the same as narcissistic abuse. That dynamic is antagonistic and manipulative. This one is self-absorbed and unavailable. Both are real and both are workable. The therapy differs.

how it works

How I Approach This Work

We name the dynamic plainly. Putting an accurate description on what your parents do makes the guilt easier to hold, because guilt thrives on the story staying vague.

Boundaries you can hold. A boundary that collapses at the first phone call is not a boundary. We build ones sized to what you can maintain under real pressure.

Guilt is expected, not a verdict. Feeling guilty after saying no is what happens when the rule was that you never do. It is not evidence that you have done something wrong.

Grief for the parent you needed. Accepting the limits of who your parents can be is a real loss, and it deserves more room than the practical logistics usually get.

Contact stays your decision. More contact, less, or none at all: I help you think it through and handle it, and I do not push you toward any particular answer.

what we work on

What I Help With

Dread before family contact. Preparing for the visit and for the days afterward, so that it costs you less than it has been costing.

Boundaries with a parent. Building limits that hold up against contact with somebody who has never once respected them before now.

Guilt after saying no. Work on the guilt that arrives right on schedule whenever you put your own needs anywhere near the front of the queue.

Role reversal and caretaking. Support when you have been the emotionally responsible one in this relationship since you were a small child.

Grieving the parent you needed. Room for the loss of the parent you did not get to have, which is real grief even though nobody has died.

Low contact and no contact. Help thinking through the decision itself, and handling the fallout from relatives once you have made it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an emotionally immature parent look like?

They struggle to tolerate or respond to other people’s feelings, including their child’s. Conversations return to them, discomfort gets deflected rather than discussed, and repair rarely happens. The child learns to manage the parent, and that arrangement usually persists into adulthood.

Why do I feel worse after visiting my parents?

Because the visit generally requires managing them, and that work does not show up in the moment. Many people get through the visit itself and then lose several days to exhaustion, irritability, or low mood afterward. It is one of the most common reasons clients contact me.

How do I set boundaries with my parents as an adult?

Start with limits you can maintain rather than ones that sound firm and collapse at the first phone call. Boundaries work better when they are specific, small enough to hold, and planned in advance. Expecting the guilt rather than being surprised by it also helps.

Is my parent emotionally immature or narcissistic?

The useful distinction is between a parent who is self-absorbed and unavailable and one whose behaviour is actively manipulative and antagonistic. I cannot diagnose someone who is not my client. What I can do is help you work with the dynamic you describe.

Should I go low contact or no contact with a parent?

That is your decision and it is rarely permanent either way. Both arrangements come with costs, from relatives, from guilt, and from what you give up. My role is helping you think it through clearly and handle the consequences of whichever you choose.
