What Is Trauma-Informed Therapy?

Trauma-informed therapy treats your symptoms in the context of what happened to you, rather than as separate problems to be managed one at a time. It starts from what you lived through, not from what is wrong with you.

Trauma can follow a single recent event, years of childhood experience, or a relationship that slowly erodes your sense of safety. The effects look similar either way: hypervigilance, trouble concentrating, sudden waves of fear, and distance from the people closest to you.

The work builds safety and practical skills first, then looks at what is driving the pattern underneath. That order matters. For many people daily life becomes manageable well before the hardest material is described in detail.

how it works

How I Approach Trauma Therapy

Safety and skills come first. Before we go anywhere near the hardest material, you get practical tools you can use in the moments when your body has already gone into full alarm.

You set the pace on detail. You never have to give me a complete account of what happened in order to work on the way that it is affecting your life right now.

We name the pattern out loud. I tell you what I am seeing rather than waiting for you to arrive at it on your own, because in my experience that is faster and more useful.

Something leaves the room with you. Every stretch of work has a practical end to it: a skill to rehearse, a boundary to test, or a hard conversation to prepare for in advance.

The body is part of the picture. Hypervigilance, broken sleep, and exhaustion are trauma symptoms rather than side issues, and we track them alongside everything else we do.

Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What does trauma-informed therapy really mean?

It means the work starts from what happened to you rather than from a list of symptoms. Your reactions are treated as things your nervous system learned, not as flaws. In practice that shapes the pace, how much choice you have over what gets discussed, and the order we take things in.

What is the difference between PTSD and trauma?

Trauma is the experience. PTSD is one specific diagnosis that can follow it, with defined criteria around intrusion, avoidance, mood, and arousal. Many people carry real trauma symptoms without meeting the full criteria for PTSD, and the work is worth doing either way.

How do I know if I have unresolved trauma?

Common signs include staying on alert long after the danger has passed, sudden waves of fear with no obvious trigger, feeling detached from people, and reacting to small things far more strongly than you would expect. If this sounds familiar, a consultation is a reasonable place to sort it out.

What happens in a trauma therapy session?

Early sessions focus on understanding your history and building skills you can use when you are activated. From there we work on the patterns underneath, at a pace you control. Sessions usually end with something concrete to practice before we next meet. That is deliberate.

Can talking about it in therapy make trauma worse?

It can, when the work moves faster than your capacity to handle it. That is exactly why safety and skills come first, and why you decide how much detail to give. If something is destabilizing you between sessions, tell me. We slow down.
